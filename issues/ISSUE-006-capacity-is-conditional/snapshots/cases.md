# Executive Summary
Skyline Plaza building A-4 collapsed during construction on 2 March 1973. The NBS investigation concluded that premature removal of supporting forms was a contributing factor. Its analysis identified the most likely initiating mode as shear failure around one or more columns in the 23rd-floor flat plate while the slab concrete was still relatively weak. [@SRC-0029]

# Engineering Mechanism
The 23rd-floor slab was not simply carrying its eventual service load. In the NBS Case 1 construction state it also carried the newly poured portion of the 24th-floor slab and formwork. NBS used an estimated average 23rd-floor concrete strength of about 1200 psi for that state, versus the 3000 psi 28-day design strength. Loss of support at one column could overload neighboring supports and allow debris impact to propagate collapse downward. [@SRC-0029]

# Evidence Gap
The exact shoring configuration at the moment of collapse could not be reconstructed with certainty; employee statements conflicted and additional shoring was installed after the event. The magazine therefore presents the NBS analyzed construction state, not an invented as-built shoring map.

# Engineer's Notebook
Concrete capacity belongs to an age, curing history and support state. A strength value on the structural drawings is not automatically available to the construction stage that happens to exist today.

# Executive Summary
The independent review panel formed after the 1976 Sau Mau Ping disaster concluded that water penetration into loose end-tipped fill created a wetted zone, reduced fill strength and led to rapid downhill movement that converted into a destructive mud avalanche. The panel considered inadequate compaction and end-tipping likely to have been widespread in older Hong Kong fill slopes. [@SRC-0030]

# Engineering Mechanism
A fill slope is not defined by its outline. Placement method and density create the soil structure that provides strength. Loose fill can wet quickly during intense rainfall; once a sufficiently deep wetted zone develops, strength can collapse and mobility can increase dramatically. The panel's stabilisation concept included recompacting at least a 3 m surface layer to a 1V:1.5H slope, with drainage, surface protection and safeguards against leaking services. [@SRC-0030]

# Evidence Gap
The review is a system-level post-disaster assessment, not a generic prescription for every fill slope. Modern design still requires site-specific material properties, groundwater/rainfall assessment and stability analysis.

# Engineer's Notebook
Ask how fill was placed, not only what material it is. End-tipped fill and engineered compacted fill may share a soil description while behaving as very different structures.

# Executive Summary
A domestic gas explosion at Ronan Point in London on 16 May 1968 blew out load-bearing wall panels at the 18th floor. The official inquiry found that the ensuing partial collapse was inherent in the building's design. NIST's later case history describes a precast wall-and-floor system with no redundancy for load redistribution after local support loss. [@SRC-0031] [@SRC-0032]

# Engineering Mechanism
The local panel was part of a direct vertical bearing stack. Once the exterior bearing wall was lost, upper floors lost support and fell; the impact of falling debris then triggered progressive collapse below. NIST characterises the central issue as lack of structural integrity and alternate load paths. This is the difference between a detail that is strong under its intended force and a system that remains stable after a local component disappears. [@SRC-0032]

# Evidence Gap
The magazine figure is a mechanism schematic, not a reconstruction of exact panel dimensions, bolt layout or gas pressure. NIST notes poor workmanship in some connections but also notes that it had little impact on the initiating event; the case should not be reduced to a workmanship story.

# Engineer's Notebook
For a critical bearing detail, ask the second question: what carries the load if this component is suddenly absent? Robustness starts where ordinary member capacity stops.

# Executive Summary
Following Hurricane Katrina, the Greater New Orleans Hurricane and Storm Damage Risk Reduction System was rebuilt as an integrated perimeter of levees, floodwalls, surge barriers, gates and pump stations. FEMA accredited the system in 2014 as reducing risk from effects associated with the 1-percent annual chance storm. USACE reported nearly 133 miles of defenses strengthened or improved and about 68 miles of interior defenses removed from direct storm-surge exposure by moving the line of defense outward. [@SRC-0033]

# Engineering Mechanism
The win is not a single heroic wall. It is system architecture: barriers reduce the surge entering vulnerable channels, perimeter defenses contain the remaining water, closure structures preserve the line, pump stations manage trapped/interior water and armoring improves resilience when overtopping occurs. The system also makes the maintenance condition explicit: risk reduction is not permanent without future lifts and modifications as settlement, subsidence and sea-level conditions evolve.

# Evidence Gap
FEMA accreditation is a defined risk-reduction determination, not a promise that New Orleans is flood-proof or that every conceivable storm is resisted. Residual risk and future elevation loss remain part of the system.

# Engineer's Notebook
For flood protection, the weakest open gate, low reach, unarmored transition or unavailable pump can matter more than the strongest levee segment. Review the closure of the whole hydraulic boundary.

# Executive Summary
The Burj Khalifa foundation is a piled raft founded on deep carbonate soils and rocks. The design process combined site investigation, numerical analysis, independent peer review and a substantial pile load-test programme. Trial piles were loaded to as much as 64 MN, and settlement predictions were subsequently compared with measurements taken during construction. [@SRC-0034]

# Engineering Mechanism
A piled raft is not simply a raft that happens to have piles underneath it. The raft, piles and ground participate together, and their interaction governs total and differential settlement. The later peer-reviewed re-assessment found that predicted average and differential settlements from two piled-raft analysis methods were in reasonable agreement with measured data near the end of construction and highlighted the importance of interpreting pile tests with reaction-pile interaction in mind. [@SRC-0035]

# Evidence Gap
The principal design source is project-authored, and the peer-reviewed re-assessment includes authors who were involved in foundation analysis and peer review. This is excellent performance evidence but not independent forensic verification. The magazine therefore avoids turning the case into a universal piled-raft recipe.

# Engineer's Notebook
Use full-scale tests to calibrate the model that actually governs the structure, then keep measuring. A pile test is evidence about a system only after test setup, group interaction and foundation load sharing are understood.
